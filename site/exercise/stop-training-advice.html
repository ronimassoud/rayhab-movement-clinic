Exercise & loading

When “stop training” is the wrong advice

Stopping is the easiest advice to give and the hardest to undo. It is sometimes right, and it is given far more often than it is right.

Reading time3 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Physiotherapist
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Complete rest reduces symptoms and reduces capacity at the same time, so the problem is often waiting when you return.
  • Strength and tendon tolerance start measurably declining within one to two weeks of unloading.
  • Most problems need the load modified, not removed: lighter, slower, shorter range, fewer sets.
  • Genuine reasons to stop exist, including fractures, acute significant injury and specific medical restrictions.

Why the advice gets given

It works in the short term. Remove the provoking load and symptoms settle, which looks like a successful intervention to both parties. It is also safe to say. Nobody is blamed for telling a patient to rest.

The cost arrives later, which is why it is so easy to miss. Six weeks off resolves the pain and leaves a tissue that tolerates less than it did before, so the ordinary life you return to is now relatively heavier.

What rest actually costs

  • Strength begins to decline within one to two weeks of unloading, faster in older adults.
  • Tendon stiffness and load tolerance fall when tendons stop being loaded, which is precisely the property you need back.
  • Confidence falls with capacity. Fear of a movement outlasts the tissue problem in a large share of cases.
  • Fitness, sleep, mood and weight all tend to move in the unhelpful direction, and all of them feed back into pain.

Modify instead of stop

There is almost always a version of the movement you can tolerate today. Finding it is most of the clinical work. The usual levers, roughly in the order we try them:

  1. Reduce the load and keep everything else.
  2. Shorten the range, avoiding the painful end of it rather than the movement.
  3. Slow it down, which removes the impact and the stretch-shortening demand.
  4. Change the variation: a leg press instead of a squat, an isometric hold instead of a full repetition.
  5. Reduce frequency and volume last, because those are what build tolerance back.

Keeping something is also worth more than it looks on paper. A person who trains three modified sessions a week through a flare-up returns to full load in a fraction of the time it takes someone who stopped.

When stopping is right

This is not an argument for training through everything. Stop, and get assessed, when any of the following apply: a suspected fracture or bone stress injury, a sudden loss of strength or function, significant swelling after a specific incident, pain that wakes you in the night or does not settle at rest, any red-flag symptom such as unexplained weight loss or fever, or an explicit restriction from a surgeon or physician after a procedure.

In those cases the question is not whether to rest but what you can still load safely elsewhere, which is almost always more than people assume.

What is not settled

How much load is optimal during a flare-up is genuinely unsettled, and it differs by tissue. Tendons have reasonable evidence for continued loading. For acute muscle injury and for some persistent pain presentations, the best early dose is less clear, and the honest answer is that it is decided case by case and reviewed.
