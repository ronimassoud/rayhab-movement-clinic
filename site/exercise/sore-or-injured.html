Exercise & loading

Sore or injured: reading the difference

The information you need is not in how much it hurts. It is in when it started, where exactly it is, and what it does over the next three days.

Reading time3 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Physiotherapist
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Ordinary muscle soreness peaks around 24 to 48 hours after a new load, is diffuse, and eases as you move.
  • Injury tends to be localised, often has a moment attached to it, and gets worse with the movement rather than better.
  • Behaviour over 72 hours separates the two more reliably than intensity does.
  • A short list of signs means getting assessed rather than monitoring: loss of strength, swelling, night pain, locking or giving way.

What ordinary soreness looks like

Delayed onset muscle soreness follows a load your tissue was not used to, particularly one with a lot of lowering under tension. It typically begins some hours later, peaks around 24 to 48 hours, and resolves within a few days.

  • It is spread across the muscle rather than at one point you can press.
  • It is often symmetrical, in both legs or both arms.
  • It feels worse in the first minutes of moving and better once you are warm.
  • Strength is slightly down but function is intact.

What injury tends to look like

Injury is usually more specific, in location and often in origin. People can frequently name the repetition.

  • You can point to it with one finger, and pressing there reproduces it.
  • It is on one side only, with no obvious reason for the asymmetry.
  • Warming up does not improve it, or improves it briefly and then it returns worse.
  • It is provoked by a specific movement or direction every time, reliably.
  • Something changed functionally: you cannot push off, raise the arm, or take full weight.

The 72-hour test

If you are unsure, do not decide on the day. Reduce load to something comfortable and watch for three days. Soreness follows a curve: up, then down, then gone. An injury tends to plateau, or to settle at rest and return identically the moment the movement comes back.

A useful second question: is it the same at 72 hours as it was at 24? Soreness should be clearly better. Something that is unchanged, or that is now bothering you during ordinary activity, deserves looking at.

When to get it seen rather than wait

Some findings do not benefit from a watch-and-see week. Book an assessment, or seek urgent care where relevant, for: an audible pop with immediate swelling, inability to bear weight, a joint that locks or gives way, visible deformity, numbness or pins and needles in a defined area, pain that wakes you from sleep, bone pain that is worse with impact and localised to one point, or any pain accompanied by fever, unexplained weight loss or a history of cancer.

Nothing on that list is common. All of it changes what the right plan is, which is why it is worth ruling out early rather than at week six.

What is not settled

There is no test, clinical or imaging, that cleanly separates benign training soreness from early tissue injury in every case. Experienced clinicians get this wrong too, which is why the plan is reviewed rather than set once. If a presentation does not behave as expected within a few weeks, that is itself a reason to reassess rather than to persist.
