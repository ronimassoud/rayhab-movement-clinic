Exercise & loading

What load actually means

Ask most people what their training load is and they will tell you what they lift. That answers roughly a quarter of the question.

Reading time4 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Physiotherapist
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Load is a dose: how hard, how much, how often, and how much recovery sits in between.
  • Tissue adapts to load that is slightly more than it is used to, then stops adapting when the demand stops changing.
  • Too little load is a problem in rehabilitation far more often than too much.
  • The same absolute weight is a heavy day for one person and a warm-up for another, so load is always relative to current capacity.

Load is a dose, not a weight

In the clinic, load means the total mechanical demand placed on a tissue over time. A 40 kg squat done twice is a small load. The same 40 kg squat done for ten sets, four days a week, with no easy weeks, is a large one. The weight did not change. The dose did.

This matters because almost every loading problem we see is a dose problem rather than an exercise-selection problem. The squat was not the issue; forty-eight hard sets in a week after six months off was.

The four dials

Any loading decision moves one of four things. Keeping them separate in your head is the single most useful habit you can build.

  • Intensity. How hard each repetition is, relative to what you could do at most. Usually expressed as a percentage of a maximum, or as how many repetitions you had left in reserve.
  • Volume. How much total work: sets multiplied by repetitions, or minutes, or distance.
  • Frequency. How often the tissue is loaded in a week.
  • Recovery. What sits between sessions. Sleep, food, stress and the other training you are doing all live here.

Progress comes from moving one dial at a time and leaving the rest alone long enough to see what happened. Move three at once and you learn nothing about which one mattered.

Load only means something relative to capacity

Capacity is how much a tissue can currently handle before it complains. It is specific: your calf can have a high capacity for walking and a low one for hopping. It also changes, in both directions, within weeks.

This is why a number on a programme sheet is close to meaningless on its own. Two people doing the same 3 sets of 10 are having completely different experiences if one of them is at 60 per cent of capacity and the other is at 98. The job of an assessment is to find out roughly where you are on that scale, so the number can be chosen rather than guessed.

Underloading is the more common mistake

The warning most people have heard is about doing too much. In rehabilitation the opposite is more common. Pain appears, activity drops, capacity falls, and the lower capacity makes the same ordinary tasks feel provocative. Rest resolves the flare-up and leaves the cause untouched.

A tendon that hurts under load generally needs load, applied carefully and progressed slowly, rather than removed. Which load, and how slowly, is the part that needs judgement.

What to do with this

  1. Write down what you are actually doing, in sets and repetitions, for two weeks. Most people are surprised, in one direction or the other.
  2. Change one dial. Give it two to three weeks.
  3. Judge it on how the tissue responds over days, not on how the session felt.
  4. If something hurts, reduce the dose rather than stopping; stopping usually costs more than it saves.

What is not settled

There is no agreed formula that converts a person's history into a safe starting load. Acute-to-chronic workload ratios were popular for predicting injury and have not held up well under scrutiny; they are a way of thinking rather than a calculator. Starting loads in practice are an informed estimate, checked against how you respond over the first fortnight and adjusted.
