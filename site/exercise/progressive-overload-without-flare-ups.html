Exercise & loading

Progressive overload without flare-ups

Everyone knows you have to add load to get stronger. Far fewer people know what to do in the week after the load is added and it did not go well.

Reading time3 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Physiotherapist
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Add roughly one variable at a time, in small increments, and hold it long enough to see the response.
  • A session that felt fine means less than three days without a flare-up afterwards.
  • Progression is not linear. Planned easy weeks are part of going up, not a break from it.
  • If a progression fails twice in a row, the problem is usually the step size, not your commitment.

The principle, stated plainly

Tissue adapts to demands slightly beyond what it is used to. Apply the same demand indefinitely and adaptation stops. Apply a far larger one and the tissue is overwhelmed before it can respond. Progressive overload is the narrow band in between, and the whole skill is finding it for the person in front of you.

Smaller steps than feel satisfying

The most common error is step size. Adding 20 per cent to a lift because it felt easy skips the stage where the tissue was supposed to adapt. Adding 5 per cent feels pointlessly cautious in week one and gets you considerably further by week twelve.

A workable default for most people rehabilitating something: increase one variable by around 5 to 10 per cent per week, and only after a clean week at the current level.

The order matters

When several things could go up, there is a sensible sequence. Earn the repetitions before the weight, and the weight before the speed.

  1. Range and control. Can you do the movement well, through the range you intend to load?
  2. Volume. More sets or repetitions at the same weight.
  3. Intensity. More weight, or a harder variation, with volume pulled back slightly.
  4. Speed and impact. Fast, reactive and plyometric work last, because it loads tissue many times the body weight in a fraction of a second.

Reading the response

The useful signal is not how the session felt. It is what the tissue did for the next two to three days. A practical rule we use with most people: symptoms that rise during a session but settle within 24 hours, and are no worse than before the following week, are acceptable. Symptoms that are still raised at 48 to 72 hours mean the dose was too big.

Some soreness is expected when load changes, particularly in the first fortnight. Pain that is sharp, that arrives earlier in the session each time, or that starts affecting sleep is a different signal and worth getting looked at.

When a progression does not hold

Go back to the last level you tolerated, stay there for a week or two longer than feels necessary, then take half the step you took last time. If that fails too, the limiting factor is probably not the load: sleep, stress, a second sport, or an irritable structure that needs a different entry point.

Failing a progression twice is information, not a verdict. It usually means the plan needs a smaller step or a different exercise, and it almost never means the tissue cannot be loaded at all.

What is not settled

The 24-hour and 72-hour rules above are clinical conventions that work well in practice; they are not derived from strong trial evidence and they are not equally valid for every structure. Nerve-related and persistent pain in particular can behave differently, and more load is not automatically the answer there.
