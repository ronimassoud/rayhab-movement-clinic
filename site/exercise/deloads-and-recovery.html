Exercise & loading

Deloads, rest and why recovery is part of the program

Training is the stimulus. Recovery is where the adaptation happens. Most stalled programs have plenty of the first and none of the second.

Reading time3 min read
Written byDr. Rayan Halimeh, Physiotherapist
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Adaptation occurs during recovery, so recovery is a training variable rather than its absence.
  • A planned easy week every four to eight weeks generally produces more progress over a quarter, not less.
  • Sleep is the single largest recovery lever, and the one most often ignored.
  • Life stress reduces how much training load you tolerate, which is why a program that worked in March can fail in October.

Why an easy week speeds things up

Load creates fatigue and fitness at the same time. Fatigue masks the fitness, which is why you often feel weakest at the end of a hard block and strongest ten days later. A deload removes the fatigue so the adaptation shows up, and it gives connective tissue, which remodels more slowly than muscle, a chance to catch up.

In practice this means a planned reduction every four to eight weeks, depending on how hard the block was and how old and how trained you are. Older adults and people returning from injury generally need them more often, not less.

How to deload without losing anything

Cut volume, keep some intensity. Roughly half the sets, at a weight that still feels like something, tends to work better than a week of nothing at all. Stopping entirely for seven days leaves many people feeling worse when they return, because they have shed fitness along with the fatigue.

  • Halve the number of hard sets and keep the exercises.
  • Keep the weight within about 10 percent and stop each set well short of failure.
  • Keep frequency the same, so the movement pattern stays grooved.
  • Do not add anything new in a deload week; it defeats the purpose.

Sleep does more than anything else you can buy

Restricted sleep reduces strength expression, slows reaction time, raises perceived effort and is associated with higher injury rates in athletes. It also raises pain sensitivity, which means the same tissue load hurts more after a bad week.

If sleep is genuinely limited, by a newborn, shift work or insomnia, the right response is to reduce the load to match rather than to push through. The program has to fit the life it sits inside.

Everything else counts as load too

The body does not keep separate accounts for work stress, exams, illness and training. All of it draws on the same capacity. This is the usual explanation for the program that worked perfectly for two months and then stopped: nothing changed in the gym, and a great deal changed outside it.

Practically, this means a program should be written with a lighter version built in, so a difficult couple of weeks reduces the dose rather than ending the habit.

What is not settled

Optimal deload frequency has no established answer and almost certainly varies by person, age and training history. Much of the recovery industry, from compression garments to ice baths to supplements, has weak or mixed evidence; cold-water immersion in particular may blunt some strength adaptations when used routinely after training. Sleep, food and sensible dose management are the parts with real support behind them.
