Posture

What posture training can actually change

The useful question is not whether posture can be fixed. It is which part of what you are looking at is structure, which part is habit, and which part is capacity.

Reading time3 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Physiotherapist
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Structural posture, set by bone shape and vertebral rotation, is not changed by exercise.
  • Habitual posture, which is the position you default to, does change, and it changes with capacity more than with reminders.
  • What reliably improves is strength, endurance in sustained positions, available range and comfort.
  • Expect a different experience of the same body rather than a different silhouette.

Three different things get called posture

Separating them resolves most of the confusion in this topic.

  • Structure. Bone shape, vertebral rotation, a structural curve, longstanding changes in older spines. Not modifiable by exercise.
  • Habit. The position you adopt without thinking about it, at a desk, standing, carrying. Modifiable.
  • Capacity. How long you can hold a position before it becomes uncomfortable. Highly modifiable, and usually the thing people actually want changed.

A person who says they want better posture almost always means they want to stop feeling sore at 4 pm, and that is a capacity target rather than an alignment one.

What does not change

Exercise does not straighten a structural scoliotic curve, and no amount of strengthening alters the shape of a vertebra. Scoliosis-specific exercise has a different and more defensible aim: influencing progression during growth, improving control and posture within the available range, and managing symptoms. That is worth doing, and it is not the same claim as correction.

Similarly, a longstanding thoracic kyphosis in an older adult will not return to a twenty-year-old's silhouette. Strength, balance and function all improve; the outline largely does not.

What does change, reliably

  1. Strength through range, particularly in the positions you spend time in, which raises the time before discomfort appears.
  2. Endurance of the trunk and shoulder girdle, which is usually what fails during a long day.
  3. Available range, when limited range was genuinely the constraint rather than an assumed one.
  4. Awareness, meaning the ability to find and hold a different position when the current one has become uncomfortable.
  5. Confidence, which sounds soft and changes behaviour more than any of the above.

Setting the expectation honestly

Programmes sold on photographs set up a disappointment that is entirely avoidable. A before-and-after image can be produced in an afternoon by standing differently, and it tells you nothing about structure or symptoms.

A better target: in eight to twelve weeks of consistent work, most people can expect more tolerance for sustained positions, more strength in the ranges they use, and less discomfort. That is a real outcome. It is simply not a photographic one.

What is not settled

How much postural habit can be changed durably, and whether changing it affects symptoms independently of the strength gained alongside it, is not well established. Most trials of postural exercise improve symptoms modestly and do not isolate whether posture or general strengthening did the work.
