Posture

Sitting, desk work and the best posture myth

Sitting has been called the new smoking, which is both inaccurate and unhelpful. The problem with a desk day is not the chair; it is that nothing changes for eight hours.

Reading time3 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Physiotherapist
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Sitting itself is not established as a cause of back pain. Prolonged static positions of any kind are the more consistent problem.
  • No single sitting posture has been shown to be protective, and expensive chairs do not resolve symptoms on their own.
  • Changing position frequently has better support than getting the position right.
  • Overall physical activity across the week matters more for back pain than desk setup does.

Is sitting actually bad for your back?

Reviews looking for an association between occupational sitting and low back pain have not found a convincing one. People with desk jobs do report back pain, and so do people with physically demanding jobs, and so do people who do neither.

What does show up repeatedly is duration. Discomfort rises with time in one position, and it falls when the position changes, regardless of which position it was. That is a different problem from sitting, and it has a different solution.

What ergonomics is worth

Ergonomic adjustment can make a workstation more comfortable, which is a reasonable goal in itself. What the evidence does not support is the idea that a correct setup prevents or cures back and neck pain; workplace ergonomic interventions on their own have produced modest and inconsistent results.

The practical implication: a chair and a monitor height that let you work comfortably are worth having, and they are not a treatment. If symptoms are persistent, the setup is rarely the whole story.

What actually helps a desk day

  1. Change position every 30 to 60 minutes. Standing, walking, leaning, a different chair; the variety is the active ingredient.
  2. Take short movement breaks rather than long infrequent ones. Two minutes of walking several times a day beats one longer break.
  3. Use a sit-stand desk as a way to add variety, not as a replacement posture. Standing all day produces its own complaints.
  4. Keep the week physically active outside work hours, which is the intervention with the strongest evidence behind it.
  5. Build strength. Tolerance for sustained positions is a capacity question as much as a positioning one.

On slouching

Slouching is not dangerous. Spines flex comfortably and are built to. Studies comparing upright and slouched sitting have not shown the upright group to be protected from pain, and intensive postural correction programmes have not outperformed general activity advice.

Where slouching becomes relevant is when it is the only position used for hours, or when it reliably reproduces a specific person's symptoms. Then it is worth addressing, as a dose problem rather than a moral one.

What is not settled

How much sitting matters for back pain specifically remains debated, and the evidence is mostly observational and confounded by overall activity levels. Prolonged sitting does have well-supported effects on general health that are separate from back pain, and those are a good enough reason to move regularly on their own.
