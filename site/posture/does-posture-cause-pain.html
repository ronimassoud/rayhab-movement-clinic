Posture

Does posture cause pain?

If bad posture reliably caused pain, we would be able to pick out the people in pain from a photograph. Repeatedly, researchers have tried, and they cannot.

Reading time3 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Physiotherapist
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Measured posture correlates weakly with pain. Many people with unusual posture are pain-free and many with textbook posture are not.
  • No posture has been shown to be the correct one, and no single position is safe indefinitely.
  • What you do with a position over time, meaning duration, load and variety, matters more than the position itself.
  • Posture can still be relevant to an individual: if a specific position reliably reproduces your symptoms, that is useful information.

What the studies actually found

The idea that postural deviation causes pain has been tested extensively, in neck pain, low back pain and shoulder pain, and the associations found are consistently weak and inconsistent between studies. Measurements such as forward head position, lumbar curvature and sitting posture do not separate people with pain from people without it reliably enough to be useful.

This is not a fringe position; it is the mainstream reading of the evidence in musculoskeletal research. It sits awkwardly alongside how confidently posture is discussed in clinics, workplaces and social media.

There is no single correct posture

Spines vary. The amount of curvature considered normal spans a wide range, and the people at either end of it are not in more pain than those in the middle. Attempts to define an ideal alignment have produced standards that most healthy, comfortable people fail.

The more defensible version of the advice is about variation rather than position: no posture is harmful briefly, and any posture becomes uncomfortable if held long enough. The problem is usually duration, not geometry.

Why the belief is so durable

  • It is visible. Posture can be seen and photographed, which makes it feel like an explanation in a way that load history does not.
  • It offers a fix. Sit up straight is actionable, and uncertainty is not.
  • Correcting posture often does reduce symptoms in the short term, usually because it changes position after a long period in one position, which would have helped regardless.
  • It has been taught for a century, which is its own form of evidence in people's minds.

When posture genuinely is relevant

Dismissing posture entirely overcorrects. It is worth attention in several specific situations.

  1. A position reliably reproduces your symptoms, and changing it reliably eases them. That is a usable finding for your case, whatever the population data says.
  2. A sustained position is part of a load problem: eight hours in one arrangement is a dose, and doses can be reduced.
  3. A structural condition is present. Scoliosis and significant kyphosis are not posture habits and are assessed differently.
  4. Posture is being used as a performance variable, in lifting or sport, where position changes the loads on tissue substantially.

What we avoid is the leap from your posture is unusual to your posture is why you hurt, because that leap is where unnecessary worry and years of pointless correction come from.

What is not settled

The weak association between static posture and pain is well established; the right amount of attention to give posture clinically is not. Reasonable clinicians differ, particularly for neck pain and for occupations with sustained fixed positions. What is clear is that the strength of the usual public advice is not supported by the data behind it.
