Posture

Schoolbags, screens and the blame they carry

Almost every parent who walks into the clinic with a newly diagnosed curve has already been told it was the bag. It was not the bag.

Reading time3 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Physiotherapist
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Carrying a schoolbag does not cause scoliosis. Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis has a genetic and developmental basis.
  • Heavy bags can cause short-term discomfort, which is a different claim from causing a structural condition.
  • Screen use is associated with neck discomfort in some studies, weakly, and no evidence shows it causes structural change.
  • Blaming a family changes nothing clinically and adds guilt to a diagnosis that is already frightening.

Where the claim comes from

It is intuitive. A heavy bag on one shoulder, a child who stands crookedly, and a curve discovered at thirteen form an obvious-looking story. The story requires only that one thing preceded another, which is how most incorrect causal beliefs are built.

Bags do not cause scoliosis

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis is a three-dimensional structural condition with a substantial genetic component and a strong relationship to growth. It involves rotation of the vertebrae, which no amount of asymmetric carrying produces. It typically appears during the growth spurt, which is when it would appear regardless of what a child carries.

Studies examining schoolbag weight and spinal curvature have not found that bags cause scoliosis. What they do find is that heavy bags can produce temporary discomfort and postural adjustment while being carried, which resolves when the bag comes off.

If a curve has been found, the useful questions are about growth remaining, curve magnitude and progression risk. The bag is not one of them.

Screens and neck pain

Prolonged screen use is associated with reported neck and shoulder discomfort in adolescents in a number of studies, and the associations are modest and complicated by sleep, activity levels and mood, all of which travel with screen time.

The popular idea of a permanently deformed neck caused by phone use is not supported. Necks tolerate flexion; they tolerate sustained anything less well. The relevant variable is how long, not whether.

What is actually worth doing

Sensible without being guilt-laden:

  • Use both straps and keep the bag reasonably light, because comfort is worth having even if nothing structural is at stake.
  • Break up long stretches in any single position, with movement rather than correction.
  • Keep children physically active, which has far better evidence behind it than any postural intervention.
  • If a curve is suspected, get it assessed properly rather than managed through bag rules.

Why the blame itself matters

Parents arrive believing they caused their child's condition by letting them carry a bag or sit badly. That belief is both wrong and costly: it adds guilt to a diagnosis, it directs effort towards irrelevant changes, and it can delay the assessment that would actually help. Correcting it is part of the clinical work, not a side conversation.

What is not settled

Schoolbag load does have some measurable short-term effects on gait and trunk position, and whether very heavy loads over years contribute to back pain in adolescents is not fully resolved. What is clear is that bags do not cause scoliosis, and that the confidence of the usual advice far exceeds the evidence for it.
