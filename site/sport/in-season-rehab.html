Sport & return to play

Rehabilitating in-season without losing the season

In-season rehabilitation is not a compromised version of real rehabilitation. It is a different problem, with a fixed calendar and non-negotiable commitments.

Reading time3 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Physiotherapist
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • The constraint is total weekly load, so something has to come out before rehabilitation goes in.
  • Cut training volume before cutting competition, and cut the least sport-specific work first.
  • Two short strength sessions a week maintain most of what you have; maintenance is a legitimate goal in-season.
  • Decide the week on how the athlete responded to the last one, not on the plan written in August.

The constraint is the week, not the willingness

An athlete mid-season already has a full load: training sessions, matches, travel, school or work. Adding rehabilitation to a full week does not produce rehabilitation; it produces a larger total load applied to an already compromised tissue, which is the mechanism behind most in-season flare-ups.

So the first conversation is subtraction. What comes out of this week to make room? If the answer is nothing, the plan will fail, and it is better to know that in week one.

What to cut, in order

  1. General conditioning that the sport already provides.
  2. Secondary training: a second sport, extra skills sessions, additional gym work unrelated to the problem.
  3. Training volume within sessions, by reducing repetitions of the provocative task rather than removing the session.
  4. Minutes played, which is the decision most athletes resist and most often the one that works.
  5. Competition itself, last, and only when the alternative is losing considerably more of the season.

Maintenance is a real goal

You rarely build maximum strength in-season, and you do not need to. Two brief, hard, low-volume sessions a week preserve most of the strength and tendon tolerance an athlete already has, at a fatigue cost small enough to survive a competitive week.

Keep them heavy and short rather than light and long. High-volume light work costs more fatigue for less adaptation, which is exactly the wrong trade in-season.

Deciding week by week

In-season plans are reviewed constantly because the inputs change constantly. A simple daily symptom score and a note of what was done is enough to make the Monday decision defensible: symptoms trending down means progress, flat means hold, rising means subtract something now rather than after the next match.

The honest framing for the athlete is that the season is being managed rather than rescued, and that the full rebuild happens in the off-season. Most athletes accept that readily when it is said out loud at the start.

What is not settled

In-season rehabilitation is under-researched compared with post-injury protocols, and most of what is practised comes from applied sports-medicine experience rather than trials. How much can be maintained on a minimal dose, and for how long, varies considerably between individuals.
