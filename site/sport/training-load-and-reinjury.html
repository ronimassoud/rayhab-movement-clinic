Sport & return to play

Why most re-injuries happen in the first month back

Clearance feels like a finish line. Statistically it is the start of the most dangerous month of the whole process.

Reading time3 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Physiotherapist
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Re-injury risk is highest in the first weeks after return, then falls.
  • Three things change at once: load jumps, supervision stops, and nobody is tracking symptoms any more.
  • Rehabilitation usually ends at the level of ordinary training, while sport demands considerably more than that.
  • A graded first month, with volume limits and a continued strength programme, is the practical answer.

The pattern

Across sports and injuries, a disproportionate share of re-injuries occur early after return, and the rate declines with time back in full training. The tissue did not become more fragile at clearance. The circumstances changed.

What changes at clearance

  • Load jumps. Controlled rehabilitation sessions are replaced by full training, which is more variable, faster, and far less predictable.
  • Supervision ends. The weekly check that caught a reaction early stops happening.
  • The strength work gets dropped. It was rehabilitation, and rehabilitation is over, so it quietly disappears from the week.
  • Chaos returns. Opponents, fatigue, weather and competitive intent reintroduce exactly the conditions rehabilitation could not simulate.

The gap between rehabilitation and sport

Most programmes finish when the athlete can train normally. Sport is not normal training: a match involves accelerations, decelerations and changes of direction at intensities no rehabilitation session reproduces, repeated while tired and while thinking about something else entirely.

Closing that gap is the last third of rehabilitation, and it is the part most often skipped. It means reactive work, unplanned movement, contact where the sport has contact, and deliberate exposure to fatigue before the limb has to perform.

How to structure the first month

  1. Cap minutes or volume for the first two to three weeks, and decide the cap in advance rather than in the moment.
  2. Keep the strength programme running at two sessions a week. This is the single most commonly dropped and most protective element.
  3. Track symptoms daily for the first month, on a simple scale. A rising trend is actionable; a single sore day is not.
  4. Build in one genuinely easy week in the first month, which is counterintuitive and well worth it.
  5. Agree in advance what would trigger a step back, so that decision is not made by a disappointed athlete on a Saturday.

What is not settled

The size of the early risk and its causes are not fully resolved, and studies differ by injury and sport. Load-monitoring methods including acute-to-chronic workload ratios have been widely adopted on thinner evidence than their popularity suggests. The direction of the advice is well supported; the precise numbers are not.
