Sport & return to play

Return criteria, not a calendar

Tissue heals on a timeline. Capacity does not. Confusing the two is how people return on schedule and get hurt in week two.

Reading time3 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Physiotherapist
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Time tells you when tissue could be ready. Testing tells you whether it is.
  • Useful criteria cover strength, side-to-side symmetry, control under fatigue, sport-specific tasks and confidence.
  • A commonly used threshold is within 10 per cent of the other side, tested when tired rather than fresh.
  • Agreeing the criteria at the start of rehabilitation prevents the argument at the end of it.

Why the calendar is the wrong instrument

Healing timeframes are real: a muscle strain, a ligament repair and a bone stress injury each have a biological minimum. What they do not have is a maximum, and nothing about reaching week six guarantees that strength, control or confidence came back with it.

Two athletes at the same six-week mark can be in entirely different places. One trained everything that did not hurt and arrives with 95 per cent of their strength. The other rested and arrives with 60 per cent. Clearing both because the calendar says so is how the second one gets re-injured.

What a criteria set actually contains

The specifics change with the injury and the sport, but the categories are consistent. A defensible set covers all five.

  • Strength. Measured, on both sides, in the positions the sport needs. Not estimated by eye.
  • Symmetry. How the injured side compares with the other, usually expressed as a percentage.
  • Control under fatigue. Landing, cutting and deceleration tested late in a session, because that is when injuries happen.
  • Sport-specific capacity. Full training volume completed, at full intensity, without a reaction.
  • Confidence. A direct question, asked honestly. Athletes who do not trust the limb move differently and get hurt more.

The numbers people use, and what they are worth

The most widely used benchmark is a limb symmetry index within 10 per cent, meaning the injured side produces at least 90 per cent of the other side. It is a reasonable floor and it has a well-known flaw: the uninjured side also detrains during months of reduced activity, so 90 per cent of a weakened reference is not as reassuring as it sounds.

Where a pre-injury measurement exists, it is the better comparison. Where it does not, expected values for the sport and position are the fallback. Either way, test when the athlete is tired, because fresh testing flatters everyone.

Write the criteria down at the start

The argument about readiness is almost always a failure of planning rather than a clash of personalities. If the criteria are agreed in week one, week ten is an objective conversation about a checklist. If they are not, it becomes the clinician's caution against the athlete's impatience, and the person with the most at stake usually wins.

This is also how a graded return gets built: not cleared or not cleared, but training with restrictions, then full training, then limited competition, then full. Each step has its own entry condition.

What is not settled

Criteria-based return is widely recommended and only moderately well evidenced. Passing a test battery reduces re-injury risk in some studies and not in others, and no battery predicts individual outcomes reliably. It is better than a calendar, which is a lower bar than it sounds, and it is not a guarantee.
