Sport & return to play

Young athletes, growth spurts and overuse

A fourteen-year-old who grew nine centimetres this year is, for training purposes, a different athlete from the one you were coaching in January.

Reading time3 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Physiotherapist
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Rapid growth temporarily changes coordination, strength relative to limb length, and tissue tolerance.
  • Growing athletes get growth-plate and apophyseal injuries that adults do not, which behave differently and need respecting.
  • Early specialisation and year-round single-sport play are associated with higher overuse injury rates.
  • The protective factors are mundane: varied sport, limited volume, real off-seasons and enough sleep.

What a growth spurt changes

Bones lengthen before muscles and tendons catch up, which transiently reduces flexibility and changes the leverage the athlete has been using for years. Coordination that was automatic becomes approximate. Strength relative to the new limb length falls even though absolute strength is rising.

None of this is pathological, and all of it matters for load. The same training week that was appropriate six months ago may now be too much, delivered through a body that controls itself slightly less well than it did.

Injuries adults do not get

The growing skeleton has weak points adults do not have. Growth plates and apophyses, the attachment sites where tendons pull on growing bone, are more vulnerable than the tendon itself.

  • Knee pain at the lower kneecap attachment and heel pain at the back of the heel are common and are bone-attachment problems, not tendon ones.
  • Persistent, localised bone pain in a young athlete deserves assessment rather than a stretching programme.
  • A young athlete with pain in a joint after a specific incident needs imaging considered, because growth-plate injuries can look unremarkable clinically.

These conditions are usually self-limiting and manageable with load adjustment, and they are not improved by being trained through blindly.

Specialisation and volume

The pattern associated with overuse injury in young athletes is a recognisable one: a single sport, year-round, often with more hours per week than the athlete's age in years, frequently across a club team and a school team at the same time, with no genuine off-season.

Playing several sports, and taking real breaks, is associated with lower overuse injury rates and no apparent cost to long-term development. This is one of the few places in sports medicine where the cautious advice and the performance advice point the same way.

What to actually do

  1. Track height every few months. A spurt is a reason to review the training week, not to stop training.
  2. Keep total organised sport within sensible weekly limits, counting every team the athlete plays for.
  3. Build in at least one day a week off and extended breaks across the year.
  4. Add strength training; properly supervised resistance training is safe for adolescents and is protective.
  5. Take persistent localised pain seriously and early, especially around joints and bony attachments.

What is not settled

The association between early specialisation and injury is reasonably consistent and largely observational, so causation is not settled and the families who specialise differ in other ways too. Specific weekly hour limits are pragmatic consensus figures rather than established thresholds.
