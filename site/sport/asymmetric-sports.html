Sport & return to play

Asymmetric sports: tennis, swimming, throwing

A career of serving on one side leaves visible marks. Most of them are adaptations rather than problems, and knowing which is which saves a lot of unnecessary worry.

Reading time3 min read
Written byDr. Rayan Halimeh, Physiotherapist
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Side-to-side differences in one-sided athletes are normal adaptations, not automatically faults.
  • The spine and shoulder adapt to repeated rotation; bone, muscle and range all change measurably.
  • Asymmetry is worth addressing when it comes with symptoms, lost range you need, or a drop in performance.
  • Correcting a useful adaptation can cost performance, so the aim is capacity on both sides rather than symmetry for its own sake.

What one-sided sport actually changes

The body is specific. Load one side thousands of times and it adapts: muscle grows on the dominant side, bone mineral density in the dominant arm of racket players is measurably higher, and the shoulder's rotation range redistributes, typically gaining external and losing internal rotation in throwing athletes.

These are adaptations to demand, not errors. A tennis player whose dominant shoulder is different from the other one is a tennis player. The question is never whether asymmetry exists; it is whether this particular asymmetry is costing something.

Rotation and the spine

Asymmetric sports are often blamed for spinal curves, and the relationship is less direct than the blame suggests. Certain sports do show a higher prevalence of scoliosis in their athletes, and that association does not establish cause; sports also select for particular body types, and the loading, growth and selection effects are difficult to separate.

What matters clinically is more practical: a spine that rotates repeatedly under load needs the capacity to control that rotation, and it needs enough exposure to the other direction that it retains the option. That is a training argument, not a reason to give up a sport.

When an asymmetry is worth doing something about

Three signals move an asymmetry from interesting to relevant.

  1. Symptoms. Pain, impingement or a loss of tolerance on the loaded side.
  2. Lost range you need. A throwing shoulder that cannot reach the positions the throw requires is a performance and injury problem at once.
  3. Performance decline. Power or control falling away on either side, including the non-dominant one, which is often simply undertrained.

Absent all three, a measured difference between sides is usually best left alone and monitored.

What we actually do about it

The aim is not a symmetrical athlete. It is an athlete with enough capacity on the non-dominant side to share the work, and enough control of rotation to do the sport repeatedly without accumulating a problem.

  • Train the non-dominant side deliberately, because the sport will not do it for you.
  • Build strength in the ranges the sport uses, especially at end range where control is usually weakest.
  • Keep the opposite rotation available with loaded, controlled work rather than passive stretching alone.
  • Manage the dose. Serving volume and throwing counts matter more than any corrective exercise.

What is not settled

Whether correcting asymmetry prevents injury in one-sided athletes is not established. There is reasonable evidence for the adaptations themselves and much weaker evidence that normalizing them reduces risk. Throwing volume limits in youth baseball are among the few load guidelines with genuine support behind them, and they are about dose rather than symmetry.
