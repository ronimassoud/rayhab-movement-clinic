Injury rehabilitation

Hip and groin pain in active people

Groin pain is one of the harder regions to attribute precisely, which is why the plan is usually built around what you can tolerate rather than around a label.

Reading time3 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Physiotherapist
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Several structures around the hip and groin produce overlapping symptoms, so precise diagnosis is often difficult.
  • Adductor-related pain is the most common presentation in kicking and change-of-direction sports.
  • Hip shape variations including cam and pincer morphology are common in people without symptoms.
  • Progressive strengthening, particularly of the adductors and the hip in general, has the best support.

Why this region is difficult

The hip joint, the adductors, the hip flexors, the abdominal wall, the pubic bone and the lower back all refer pain into a small area, and more than one is frequently involved at the same time. Clinical tests for the region overlap considerably, and imaging finds variations in nearly everybody.

Clinical groupings used in practice are deliberately descriptive rather than structural: adductor-related, iliopsoas-related, inguinal-related, pubic-related, and hip-joint-related. They are useful because they point towards what to load, which is what the plan needs.

What usually causes it

  • Adductor-related groin pain, the most common in football and sports with kicking and cutting. Tender adductors, pain with squeezing the legs together.
  • Hip flexor related pain, often with sprinting and kicking, and frequently alongside the adductors.
  • Hip joint related pain, typically deep in the groin, worse with deep flexion and rotation, sometimes with clicking or catching.
  • Bone stress and other causes, uncommon but important, which is why a persistent groin problem that is not responding deserves reassessment rather than more of the same.

Hip shape findings, and what they mean

Cam and pincer morphology, the bony shapes associated with femoroacetabular impingement, are found commonly in people with no hip symptoms at all, and at high rates in athletes in several sports. Having the shape is not the same as having the syndrome, which requires symptoms, clinical signs and imaging findings together.

Where the full syndrome is present, both exercise-based management and surgery are used, and trials comparing them show benefit from both with no decisive advantage for surgery. A properly run loading programme first is reasonable for most people.

What rehabilitation involves

  1. Adductor strength is the priority in most athletic groin pain, and the Copenhagen adduction exercise has the best supporting evidence of any single exercise here, for both treatment and prevention.
  2. Whole-hip strength, including abductors, extensors and flexors, through the range the sport needs.
  3. Trunk and pelvic control, because the region transfers load between the legs and the torso.
  4. Graded return to sport-specific work: kicking, cutting and sprinting progressed deliberately rather than resumed.
  5. Patience. Groin problems commonly take two to four months, and longer when they have been present for a year.

What is not settled

Diagnostic categories for groin pain are consensus-based descriptions, not validated entities, and clinicians disagree in individual cases. Long-term outcomes for femoroacetabular impingement, including whether it leads to osteoarthritis and whether surgery changes that, are not resolved. Adductor strengthening has good evidence; most of the rest of the management is reasoned from principle.
