Injury rehabilitation

Neck and shoulder pain that keeps coming back

Recurrent neck and shoulder pain is one of the most common reasons people come in, and one of the most over-explained conditions in musculoskeletal health.

Reading time3 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Physiotherapist
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Most neck pain is not attributable to a specific structure, and that is not a failure of the assessment.
  • Rotator cuff related shoulder pain responds to progressive loading at least as well as to surgery in several trials.
  • Scan findings in both regions are common in people with no symptoms at all.
  • Recurrence is usually a dose and capacity pattern, and it responds to strength work plus honest load management.

The neck, and what is usually going on

The majority of neck pain is classified as non-specific, meaning no single structure can be confidently identified as the source. That label frustrates people and it is more honest than inventing a cause. It also carries a reassuring implication: non-specific neck pain generally has a good prognosis and responds to exercise and activity.

Where symptoms travel down the arm with numbness, pins and needles or weakness in a defined pattern, that is a different presentation, needs examining properly, and may involve a nerve root. It is much less common than the headline fear suggests.

The shoulder, and the surgery question

Rotator cuff related shoulder pain, which used to be called impingement, is the most common shoulder presentation. The important finding of the last decade is that progressive exercise produces outcomes comparable to subacromial decompression surgery in randomised trials, and that the surgery performed little better than placebo surgery.

That does not make surgery never appropriate. It does mean a proper loading programme, run for long enough, is a reasonable first choice for most people, and that a scan finding of a degenerative tear is not on its own an argument for an operation.

What scans show in people who are fine

  • Degenerative changes in the neck are present in a large share of people with no neck pain, and the proportion rises steadily with age.
  • Rotator cuff tears are found in many pain-free shoulders, particularly over the age of sixty.
  • Labral and bursal findings appear routinely in people with no symptoms.

This is why imaging is requested for a reason rather than for reassurance. A scan that finds something normal-for-your-age can take months to recover from psychologically, and it frequently changes nothing about the plan.

Why it keeps coming back

The usual pattern: symptoms appear, activity is reduced, symptoms settle, activity resumes, symptoms return. Strength work is either never started or stopped as soon as the pain eased, which is roughly halfway through the process that would have prevented the next episode.

  1. Build strength in the shoulder girdle and neck, loaded properly, for at least twelve weeks.
  2. Include end-range and overhead work if your life or sport needs those positions.
  3. Manage the dose: sustained positions, sudden increases in overhead volume, and new training on top of a full week are the usual triggers.
  4. Continue the programme after the pain goes. This is the step everybody skips and the step that prevents recurrence.

What is not settled

Which exercises work best for either region is not established; several quite different programmes produce similar results. The role of the shoulder blade and of specific muscle timing has been heavily emphasised historically on weak evidence. Manual therapy helps some people in the short term and does not change long-term outcomes on its own.
