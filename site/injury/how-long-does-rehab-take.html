Injury rehabilitation

How long does rehabilitation actually take?

Everybody asks this in the first session, and they deserve a real answer rather than a reassuring one.

Reading time3 min read
Written byDr. Rayan Halimeh, Physiotherapist
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Different tissues heal on different timelines; tendon and bone are considerably slower than muscle.
  • Pain usually improves before capacity does, which is why people stop too early and recur.
  • Most persistent problems need eight to twelve weeks of consistent work for durable change, and often longer.
  • The strongest predictors of a slower recovery are how long it has already been going on, sleep, and how much you can keep doing.

Tissue healing is the floor, not the answer

Biological healing gives a minimum. Muscle strains resolve in weeks, with the specific muscle and the severity mattering. Tendons remodel over months. Bone stress injuries typically need six to twelve weeks before loading can progress normally, and longer at certain high-risk sites. Ligaments vary widely.

Healing is necessary and not sufficient. Rebuilding the capacity that was lost while the tissue healed, which is a separate process, usually takes longer than the healing did.

Pain improves first, and that is a trap

The usual sequence is pain, then function, then capacity. People typically feel substantially better around weeks three to six and conclude they are better. Strength and tolerance at that point are frequently still well below where they started, which is why resuming full activity then produces the familiar recurrence.

This is the single most common reason rehabilitation fails: not a bad program, but a program stopped at the point it felt unnecessary.

Realistic ranges

  • Acute, straightforward problem, caught early: often two to six weeks.
  • Persistent problem of a few months: usually eight to twelve weeks of consistent work before the gains are stable.
  • Problem present for a year or more: three to six months is a realistic expectation, with progress visible much earlier.
  • Return to high-demand sport after a significant injury: months, decided by criteria rather than dates.
  • Scoliosis and other structural conditions: managed over years rather than resolved, with the aim stated honestly in each plan.

These are ranges, and anybody giving you a single number for your case before assessing you is guessing.

What makes it faster or slower

  1. Duration so far. The best single predictor. Longstanding problems take longer, which is the argument for coming in earlier rather than later.
  2. What you can keep doing. People who maintain some loading throughout recover considerably faster than people who stop.
  3. Consistency. Three sessions a week for ten weeks beats daily work for two weeks and then nothing.
  4. Sleep and stress, both of which change pain sensitivity and recovery capacity directly.
  5. Other health factors, including diabetes, smoking, inflammatory conditions and age, all of which affect tissue healing rates.
  6. Expectations. What a person believes about their recovery is a measured predictor of their outcome, which is a reason to be accurate rather than either optimistic or grim.

What is not settled

Prognosis in musculoskeletal conditions is genuinely imprecise. Prediction models exist for low back pain and some other conditions and perform modestly at the individual level. The ranges above come from clinical practice and typical presentations, and individual recoveries fall outside them in both directions regularly.
