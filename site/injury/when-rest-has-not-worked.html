Injury rehabilitation

When rest alone has not worked

Rest removes the demand. It does not raise the ceiling. So when the demand returns, so does the problem, and nothing in between changed that.

Reading time3 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Physiotherapist
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Rest reduces symptoms by removing load, and simultaneously lowers how much load you tolerate.
  • If a problem returns every time you resume activity, the limiting factor is capacity, which rest cannot build.
  • Active rehabilitation works by raising tolerance gradually, which means some loading from early on.
  • Persistent problems usually need eight to twelve weeks of consistent work before the gains are stable.

The cycle most people arrive in

It is recognisable within a minute of hearing the history. Something hurts, so activity stops. The pain settles, which confirms that stopping was right. Activity resumes at roughly the previous level, the pain returns, and the conclusion drawn is that the tissue is damaged and needs more rest.

Each round of this leaves capacity lower than the round before. By the third or fourth cycle, tasks that were never a problem are provocative, and the person reasonably concludes they are getting worse.

What rest cannot do

Rest is genuinely useful for a short period after an acute injury, when the tissue needs protecting. What it cannot do is increase tolerance, because tolerance is built by load. A tendon, a muscle and a joint all become more capable in response to demand, and no amount of time off supplies that demand.

So the question is never rest or no rest. It is which load, at what dose, starting when.

What active rehabilitation actually involves

  1. Find the entry point. Something you can do today without a flare-up. There almost always is one, even if it is an isometric hold or a very short range.
  2. Establish a baseline. Two weeks at that level, consistently, so there is something to progress from.
  3. Progress deliberately, one variable at a time, roughly weekly, with a rule for what to do if it does not hold.
  4. Keep doing it longer than the symptoms last. Symptoms improve before capacity does, and stopping at symptom relief is why problems recur.
  5. Measure something. Strength, range, a tolerated time or distance. Without a number, progress is a feeling and feelings fluctuate.

What the first month looks like

Weeks one and two usually feel like very little is happening, which is the hardest part to sit with. Weeks three and four are typically when the first clear change shows up, often in function before pain. Expect the symptom graph to be jagged rather than a clean descent; a bad day inside an improving month is normal.

If there is genuinely no change by week four or five, that is a reason to reassess rather than to persist harder. Either the diagnosis, the dose or something outside the programme needs revisiting.

What is not settled

Exercise-based rehabilitation has good evidence across most common musculoskeletal problems, and which exercise, at what dose, is far less settled. For several conditions, including shoulder and knee pain, different programmes produce broadly similar results, which suggests that doing something progressive and sticking with it matters more than the specific selection.
