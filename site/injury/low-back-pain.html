Low back pain: what the scan does and does not explain

A report full of alarming words describes a spine that looks much like everybody else's. That is the single most useful thing to know about back imaging.

Reading time3 min read
Written byDr. Rayan Halimeh, Physiotherapist
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Disc degeneration and bulges are present in a majority of pain-free adults by middle age.
  • Around 90 percent of low back pain is non-specific, meaning no single structure can be blamed.
  • Imaging early, without a clinical reason, is associated with worse outcomes rather than better ones.
  • A small list of red-flag symptoms genuinely does need prompt medical assessment.

What scans show in people with no pain

This is the finding that reframes the whole topic. In large studies of adults without any back pain, disc degeneration is present in roughly a third of people in their twenties and in the large majority by their sixties. Disc bulges, height loss and facet changes follow the same pattern.

So finding degeneration on your scan establishes that you have a spine of your age. It does not establish that the degeneration is producing your symptoms, and the correlation between the severity of findings and the severity of pain is weak.

Non-specific does not mean unexplained

Most low back pain is classified as non-specific. The term means that a single pain-producing structure cannot be reliably identified, which is different from saying nothing is happening or that the pain is imagined.

What can usually be identified is a pattern: which movements and positions provoke and ease it, what the recent load history was, how much capacity the trunk and hips currently have, and what else is going on in the person's life. That pattern is what a plan is built from, and it is more useful than a structural label would be.

Why early imaging can make things worse

Guidelines across countries advise against routine imaging for low back pain without red flags, and the reason is not cost alone. Patients who are imaged early tend to have more medical care, more procedures and no better outcomes. The findings cause worry, the worry reduces activity, and reduced activity is the one thing that reliably makes back pain worse.

When it does need investigating

Seek prompt medical assessment, urgently where indicated, for: loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness in the saddle area between the legs, progressive weakness in the legs, pain following significant trauma, unexplained weight loss, fever, a history of cancer, or severe unremitting night pain. These are uncommon and they change the plan completely.

What actually helps

  1. Stay as active as symptoms allow. Bed rest makes outcomes worse, consistently.
  2. Exercise, of almost any kind, done progressively. The specific type matters much less than doing it.
  3. Build trunk and hip strength over months, not weeks.
  4. Understand what is happening, because explanation measurably reduces fear and fear measurably reduces activity.
  5. Address sleep and stress, which change pain sensitivity directly.

What is not settled

Why some back pain becomes persistent and some resolves is not well understood, and no treatment performs dramatically better than the alternatives. Effect sizes for exercise, manual therapy and education are all modest. The strongest claims in this field, in both directions, outrun the evidence; what is firmly established is that imaging findings are common in pain-free people and that activity is better than rest.
