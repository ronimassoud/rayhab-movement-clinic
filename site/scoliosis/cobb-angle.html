Pillar 01 · What scoliosis is

Reading your Cobb angle: what the number does and doesn't tell you

The Cobb angle is the number everyone remembers from the appointment. It is worth knowing exactly what it measures, and what it leaves out.

Reading time3 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Certified Schroth Practitioner
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • The Cobb angle measures the sideways tilt between the two most tilted vertebrae of a curve.
  • Repeat measurements of the same X-ray commonly differ by around 5 degrees, so a small change may be measurement noise.
  • It says nothing about rotation, appearance, pain or function.
  • A single number means little. The direction of travel over time means a great deal.

What the measurement actually is

To measure a Cobb angle, the clinician finds the vertebra at the top of the curve that tilts most, and the vertebra at the bottom that tilts most. A line is drawn along each of those two vertebrae, and the angle where those lines would meet is the Cobb angle.

It is a measure of one thing: the sideways tilt between the two ends of a curve, on a flat image, taken while standing. That is genuinely useful, because it is reproducible enough to compare over time and it is the number that treatment thresholds are built around.

Why 5 degrees is not always a change

Choosing which vertebrae are the most tilted involves judgement, and small differences in how a patient stands change the image. As a result, the same X-ray measured twice, or measured by two clinicians, commonly differs by around 5 degrees.

This has a direct practical consequence. A curve reported as 24 degrees and later as 28 degrees may have progressed, or may be the same curve measured slightly differently on a slightly different day. This is why clinicians look for a trend across several reviews rather than reacting to a single reading.

What the number leaves out

  • Rotation. Two curves measuring 30 degrees can look completely different on the body, because one is far more rotated than the other.
  • Appearance. Shoulder level, waist asymmetry and rib prominence do not track the Cobb angle neatly.
  • Pain. There is no reliable relationship between curve size and pain in adolescents.
  • Function. What you can do, lift, and tolerate is not predicted by the angle.

This is why an assessment measures more than the angle, and why two people with the same number can need quite different programmes.

The thresholds you will hear

Certain numbers recur in conversations about scoliosis. They are useful landmarks rather than rules, and they are always weighed against how much growing is left.

  • 10 degrees, the point at which a curve is called scoliosis at all.
  • 20 to 25 degrees in a growing spine, where active treatment is usually discussed.
  • 40 to 45 degrees and above, where surgical opinion is commonly sought.

The same angle carries very different weight at different ages. Twenty-five degrees in a child with years of growth ahead is a different situation from 25 degrees in an adult whose spine stopped growing a decade ago.

What is not settled

There is no agreed threshold at which exercise-based treatment stops being worthwhile, and practice varies between clinics and countries. Where your curve sits relative to these landmarks is a starting point for a conversation, not an automatic decision.
