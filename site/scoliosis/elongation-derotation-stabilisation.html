Pillar 02 · The Schroth Method

Elongation, de-rotation, stabilisation: the three jobs

Once you can see which of the three jobs an exercise is doing, the whole programme stops looking arbitrary.

Reading time2 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Certified Schroth Practitioner
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Elongation creates space and reduces the curve before any other correction is attempted.
  • De-rotation turns the trunk against the rotation of the curve.
  • Stabilisation holds the result with muscular effort so it survives outside the session.
  • The order matters: de-rotating a collapsed spine achieves much less than de-rotating a lengthened one.

Elongation comes first

A curve shortens the spine. Actively lengthening, growing tall through the crown of the head while the pelvis stays anchored, reduces the curve before anything else is added, and creates the space in which the other corrections become possible.

This is an active effort, not someone pulling on your head. The distinction matters, because the aim is a correction you can produce yourself on a bus.

De-rotation

With length established, the trunk is turned against the direction of the curve's rotation. In practice this is a combination of shoulder girdle position, ribcage orientation and pelvic position, and it is specific to the pattern: which way to turn depends entirely on which way the curve rotates.

This is the step that most obviously cannot be generic.

Stabilisation

A correction that collapses the moment attention wanders has not achieved much. Stabilisation is the muscular work of holding the corrected position, first briefly, then for longer, then while breathing normally, then while moving, then while carrying something.

This is also where the programme stops being about posture and starts being about strength and endurance, which is why scoliosis rehabilitation and strength training are not separate projects.

Putting them together

A mature correction has all three running at once: long, turned, and held, with breath directed into the areas that are collapsed. That is a lot to coordinate, which is why it is taught in pieces and assembled gradually.

When a programme is reviewed, the question is usually which of the three has quietly dropped out. Elongation is the most common casualty.
