Pillar 04 · The assessment

Your child was just diagnosed. What now?

Almost nothing about scoliosis is an emergency. The most useful thing you can do in the first week is gather information and slow down.

Reading time2 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Certified Schroth Practitioner
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Get the four facts: curve size, curve location, skeletal maturity, and date of measurement.
  • Most curves do not need surgery and many need only monitoring.
  • Bracing decisions are time-sensitive in a growing child. That part should not drift.
  • Avoid searching image results. They show the extreme end and are not representative.

The four facts to write down

Before doing anything else, make sure you have these, in writing, from whoever made the diagnosis.

  1. The Cobb angle, and which curve it refers to if there is more than one.
  2. The location and direction, for example a right thoracic curve.
  3. Skeletal maturity, usually the Risser grade, or an estimate of remaining growth.
  4. The date the X-ray was taken, since everything later is compared against it.

With those four, any clinician can orient themselves quickly. Without them, every new appointment starts from nothing.

Some perspective, quickly

Around 2 to 3 in 100 adolescents have a curve. The large majority never require surgery, and many never require active treatment at all beyond periodic review. The images that come up in a search are the severe end of a wide spectrum.

Your child is very unlikely to be at that end, and being shown those pictures in the first week is a specific, avoidable harm.

What is genuinely time-sensitive

One thing does deserve urgency. If the curve is in the range where bracing may be indicated and the child is still growing, that decision sits in a window that closes as growth finishes. Delaying it by months to think it over can remove the option.

Everything else, choosing a clinic, starting exercise, reading, second opinions, can be done at a sensible pace.

Questions worth asking

  • Given the size and the remaining growth, what is the realistic risk of progression?
  • When should this be re-measured, and by whom?
  • Is bracing indicated now, or is there a threshold at which it would be?
  • What would change the plan?

Talking to your child about it

Plainly, and without catastrophe. Most adolescents cope well when they know what the curve is, what the plan is, and what would change it. They cope badly with overheard anxiety and vague dread.

It also helps to say early that this is something they will end up managing themselves, because that is true and it sets the expectation from the start.
