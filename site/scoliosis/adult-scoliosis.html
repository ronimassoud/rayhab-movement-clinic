Pillar 01 · What scoliosis is

Scoliosis in adults: two different stories

Adults with scoliosis fall into two broad groups, and mixing them up leads to a lot of unnecessary worry.

Reading time3 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Certified Schroth Practitioner
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Adult idiopathic scoliosis is a curve carried since adolescence into adult life.
  • Degenerative scoliosis is a new curve that develops as the spine ages, usually in the lower back.
  • Adults usually seek help for pain and function rather than for the angle.
  • Meaningful improvement in symptoms and capacity is realistic even when the curve itself does not change much.

The curve you grew up with

Many adults have had a curve since their teens. It may have been monitored and discharged, or never formally assessed at all. These curves are usually stable, especially below roughly 45 to 50 degrees, and the reason for seeking help is typically discomfort, fatigue with prolonged postures, appearance, or simply wanting to know where things stand.

The goals here are about capacity and management. Building strength, improving how the trunk is controlled under load, and learning what to do when a flare happens are all realistic. Substantially changing the angle of a mature curve is not the aim.

The curve that appeared later

Degenerative, or de novo, scoliosis develops in adulthood, most often in the lower back, as discs lose height unevenly and the facet joints and ligaments change. Someone with no history of scoliosis in adolescence can develop a measurable curve in their fifties or later.

Symptoms here often have as much to do with the associated degenerative changes as with the curve: back pain, leg pain, and symptoms that come on with standing and walking and ease with sitting or bending forward.

What rehabilitation is aiming at

  • Tolerance, being able to stand, walk and work for longer before symptoms interrupt.
  • Strength and endurance, particularly through the trunk and hips.
  • Movement options, so there is more than one way to perform the tasks that provoke symptoms.
  • Self-management, knowing what a flare is and what to do about it.

None of those are angle measurements, which is exactly the point. For most adults the angle is not the thing that is limiting their life.

When to seek a broader opinion

Progressive leg weakness, numbness in the saddle area, changes in bladder or bowel function, or leg symptoms that are worsening steadily all warrant prompt medical assessment rather than a rehabilitation plan alone.

What is not settled

Evidence for exercise-based treatment in adult scoliosis is thinner than in adolescents, and most of what exists concerns symptoms and function rather than the curve. That is an honest limitation, not a reason to do nothing: exercise for back pain and for general capacity is well supported in its own right.
