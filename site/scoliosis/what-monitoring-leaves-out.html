Pillar 04 · The assessment

What “monitoring” leaves out

Many people leave a diagnosis with one instruction: come back in six months for another X-ray. That is a plan, and often a sensible one. It is also narrower than it sounds.

Reading time3 min read
Written byDr. Rayan Halimeh, Certified Schroth Practitioner
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Monitoring answers one question well: is the Cobb angle changing fast enough to change the treatment?
  • It tracks a single number, measured on a flat image, with an error of about 5 degrees either way.
  • It says little about rotation, posture, strength, breathing, pain or how you move, and nothing about the months between visits.
  • Watching and working can happen together. Ask what the plan is for the time in between.

What monitoring is for

For a small curve in a growing spine, the standard first step is often to watch it: repeat X-rays every four to twelve months, depending on how much growth is left and how large the curve is. The purpose is specific. If the curve grows past a threshold while there is still growth ahead, the plan changes, usually toward bracing.

That is a reasonable approach, because many small curves never progress, and treating every one of them aggressively would mean treating many people who did not need it. Monitoring exists to find the ones that do.

It follows one number

A monitoring visit is usually built around the Cobb angle: the tilt between the most-tilted vertebrae, measured on a standing X-ray. It is the number treatment thresholds are built on, so it matters. It is also a two-dimensional measurement of a three-dimensional change, and two readings of the same image can differ by about 5 degrees.

So a change of 3 or 4 degrees between visits may be measurement rather than movement. A change of 5 degrees or more, in the same direction, on comparable images, is what is generally treated as real progression.

What it does not look at

  • Rotation, the part of a curve people actually see as a rib or waist asymmetry, which a frontal X-ray captures poorly.
  • Posture and trunk balance, and how they change across the day and under load.
  • Strength, endurance and mobility, especially around the trunk and hips.
  • Breathing pattern, which a curve can change and which can be trained.
  • Pain, confidence and activity: whether you are still doing the sports and the daily things you want to do.

None of these appear in a Cobb angle, and all of them can be assessed without radiation.

The months in between

The quieter problem is that monitoring, on its own, assigns nothing to do between visits. For some families that is reassuring. For others it is the hardest part: six months of waiting for a number, with no sense of whether anything they do makes a difference.

For mild curves in growing adolescents, international scoliosis guidelines include scoliosis-specific exercise as an option alongside observation, aimed at limiting progression and improving posture, function and how a person feels about their back. It does not replace monitoring. It gives the time between X-rays a purpose.

What is not settled

Whether exercise during the monitoring period changes the long-term course of a mild curve is not settled. Some trials have reported less progression with scoliosis-specific exercise, but the evidence is limited and of low certainty. What is better supported is that it is safe, and that it can improve posture, strength and quality of life.

What to ask at a monitoring appointment

  1. What is the Cobb angle today, and how does it compare with the last image?
  2. How much growth is likely to be left?
  3. What change would alter the plan, and what would happen then?
  4. When is the next X-ray, and why that interval?
  5. Is there anything we should be doing until then?

The last question is the one most often left unasked. A good answer to it is the difference between waiting and working.
