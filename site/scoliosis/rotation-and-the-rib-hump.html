Pillar 01 · What scoliosis is

Rotation, and why the rib hump is not the whole story

When someone notices scoliosis in a mirror or a photograph, what they are usually seeing is rotation.

Reading time3 min read
Written byDr. Rayan Halimeh, Certified Schroth Practitioner
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Rotation is the vertebrae turning around the spine's long axis, carrying the ribs with them.
  • It is measured on the body as trunk rotation, usually with a scoliometer during a forward bend.
  • Rotation and Cobb angle are related but do not move together reliably.
  • Appearance concerns are usually rotation concerns, which is why they deserve their own attention.

What rotation is

Alongside bending sideways, the vertebrae in a curve turn about the vertical axis of the spine. In a thoracic curve that turning carries the attached ribs backward on one side and forward on the other, producing a prominence on one side of the back and a flattening on the other.

The forward bend test

Rotation is hard to see when standing and obvious when bending forward, because the ribs on the rotated side rise. This is why the forward bend test is the standard screening maneuver, and why a curve is so often first noticed at a swimming pool or during a physiotherapy appointment for something else.

The amount of rotation is measured with a scoliometer, a simple inclinometer laid across the back during the forward bend. It reports trunk rotation in degrees. A reading of around 7 degrees is a commonly used threshold for referring on for imaging, though thresholds vary.

Why it does not track the Cobb angle

Trunk rotation measured on the body and Cobb angle measured on an X-ray are two different measurements of two different things. They are related, but loosely. A curve can be relatively large on X-ray and modest on the body, or the reverse.

This matters because the two numbers answer different questions. The Cobb angle is what progression thresholds are built on. Trunk rotation is much closer to what a patient actually sees and minds.

Why rotation gets its own attention

When people describe what bothers them about scoliosis, they rarely describe an angle. They describe a shoulder blade that sticks out, a waist that is uneven on one side, a bra strap that slips. Those are rotation, and they are legitimate reasons to seek help even when the Cobb angle is modest.

It is also why treating a curve as a flat, sideways problem misses the part patients care about most.

What is not settled

How much rotation can be changed by exercise, and how durable any change is, remains an open question. Improvements in trunk rotation have been reported in studies of curve-specific exercise, but the quality of evidence is limited and results vary between individuals.
