Pillar 01 · What scoliosis is

Idiopathic, congenital, neuromuscular: why the type matters

Scoliosis is not one condition. The word describes a shape, and several very different situations produce that shape.

Reading time2 min read
Written byDr. Rayan Halimeh, Certified Schroth Practitioner
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Idiopathic scoliosis has no identified cause and accounts for roughly 80 percent of cases.
  • Congenital scoliosis comes from vertebrae that formed differently before birth.
  • Neuromuscular scoliosis develops alongside a condition affecting muscle control.
  • Degenerative scoliosis appears in adulthood as the spine ages, and behaves differently from all of the above.

Idiopathic

This is the common one. Idiopathic means no cause has been identified, and it accounts for around 80 percent of scoliosis. It is usually subdivided by the age at which it appears, with adolescent idiopathic scoliosis being by far the largest group.

Idiopathic scoliosis typically appears during the growth spurt, in someone otherwise healthy, with a normal neurological examination. The absence of a cause is not a gap in your particular investigation; it is the current state of knowledge for the condition as a whole.

Congenital

Here the vertebrae themselves formed differently during early development, before birth. A vertebra may be partly formed, or two may be joined on one side, so the spine grows unevenly from the start.

Congenital curves behave according to the underlying bone anatomy, so the reasoning behind treatment is different. They are also more often associated with differences in other systems that developed at the same time, which is why further investigation is usual.

Neuromuscular

When the muscles that support the trunk do not work typically, the spine can curve as a consequence. This occurs alongside conditions such as cerebral palsy, muscular dystrophy and spina bifida.

The priorities here are often different: comfortable sitting, breathing, skin integrity and care. Goals are set around function and daily life rather than around the angle alone.

Degenerative, or adult-onset

This is a curve that was not there in adolescence and develops later as discs and joints change with age, most often in the lower back. It behaves quite differently from a curve someone has carried since they were fourteen, and the reasons people seek help are usually pain and function rather than appearance or progression.

Why the distinction matters to you

Exercise-based scoliosis rehabilitation, including Schroth, was developed and studied mainly in idiopathic curves. Applying it to another type is not automatically wrong, but the reasoning, the goals and the realistic expectations all change.

If the type of your scoliosis has never been stated clearly, it is a fair thing to ask. It is the first fork in the road.
