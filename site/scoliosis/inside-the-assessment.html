Pillar 04 · The assessment

What happens in a 60-minute assessment

Knowing what the hour contains makes it easier to arrive prepared and to judge afterward whether it was worth it.

Reading time2 min read
Written byDr. Rayan Halimeh, Certified Schroth Practitioner
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • The appointment is an hour, and most of it is assessment rather than treatment.
  • Posture, curve pattern, trunk rotation, mobility, strength and breathing are all examined.
  • You leave with findings explained and a plan, not a diagnosis withheld for next time.
  • Bring any imaging you already have. You do not need to obtain new imaging first.

The conversation first

It starts with history: when the curve was noticed, by whom, what has been measured before, what treatment has happened, what activities matter to you, and what specifically brought you in. That last answer shapes everything, because the plan for someone worried about progression differs from the plan for someone whose back aches by the evening.

Looking and measuring

Then the physical assessment. This is done in clothing that allows the back to be seen, so bring something you can be assessed and move in.

  • Standing posture from behind and the side, noting shoulder and pelvis level and how the trunk sits over the pelvis.
  • Forward bend with a scoliometer, measuring trunk rotation in degrees.
  • Curve pattern, establishing where the apex sits and which way it rotates.
  • Photographs against a grid, with your consent, so later comparison is against a record rather than a memory.
  • Any existing X-rays, reviewed and the Cobb angle confirmed.

Watching you move

Static posture is only part of it. Spinal and hip mobility, the ability to actively correct and hold, trunk strength and endurance, breathing pattern and chest expansion, and how the trunk behaves under load and when fatigued all get examined.

For athletes this extends into sport-specific positions, because the plan has to work where the loading actually happens.

The explanation

Findings are explained during the appointment, with the images and numbers in front of you. This part matters. A number you do not understand is not information, and people manage conditions they understand considerably better.

What you leave with

  1. A clear statement of what was found and what it means.
  2. An initial home program, taught rather than handed over on paper.
  3. A review point, and what will be re-measured at it.
  4. Where relevant, a recommendation about onward referral, such as for imaging or a bracing opinion.

If a curve is outside what rehabilitation should be managing alone, you will be told that at the first appointment rather than after a course of treatment.
