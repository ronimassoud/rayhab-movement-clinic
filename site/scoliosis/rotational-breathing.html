Pillar 02 · The Schroth Method

What rotational breathing actually is

Of everything in a Schroth programme, this is the part people find hardest to believe and, once learned, hardest to unlearn.

Reading time2 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Schroth Level 2
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Rotation changes the shape of the ribcage, collapsing it on one side and expanding it on the other.
  • Rotational breathing directs air into the collapsed areas to expand them from within.
  • It is a learned motor skill, taught with hands, mirrors and cues, not a breathing exercise in the relaxation sense.
  • It is used in combination with an active correction, not on its own.

The problem it addresses

When vertebrae rotate, the attached ribs go with them. On one side the ribs are pushed backwards and splay apart; on the other they are drawn forwards and crowd together. The result is a ribcage that is no longer symmetrical, with regions that have become habitually collapsed and move very little.

Those collapsed regions are not passive bystanders. They are part of what holds the shape in place.

The idea

Breathing is not evenly distributed by necessity. With training, a person can bias where expansion happens: more into the back than the front, more into one side than the other, more into the lower ribs than the upper. Rotational breathing uses that to expand the collapsed areas from the inside while the rest of the trunk is held in a corrected position.

You are, in effect, using air as a corrective force in a direction that muscles cannot easily reach.

How it is taught

Almost always through touch first. A hand or a strap gives the target something to push against, so there is clear feedback about whether the right area moved. Mirrors, tape and verbal cues are added. Most people cannot do it on the first attempt and can do something recognisable by the end of the first session.

It is then combined with the correction, because breathing into a collapsed area while the trunk is slumped simply reinforces the existing shape.

What it feels like

Effortful and slightly odd. People commonly report that the corrected side feels stretched or unfamiliar, and that a few breaths are tiring in a way that ordinary breathing is not. That fades as the skill becomes more automatic.

What is not settled

Rotational breathing is a core component of Schroth practice, but it has not been isolated and tested on its own. What has been studied is whole programmes that include it, so its individual contribution is not separately established.
