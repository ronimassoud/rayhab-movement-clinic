Pillar 01 · What scoliosis is

Curve patterns: thoracic, lumbar and double curves

Two people can both have a 30-degree curve and need entirely different programmes, because the curve is in a different place.

Reading time2 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Certified Schroth Practitioner
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Curves are described by where the apex sits: thoracic, thoracolumbar, lumbar, or double.
  • Thoracic curves involve the ribs, so they show as rib prominence and shoulder asymmetry.
  • Lumbar curves more often show as an uneven waist and a hip that looks higher.
  • Double curves can look more balanced from behind while still being substantial.

How curves are named

A curve is named by the region containing its apex, the vertebra furthest from the midline, and by the side the curve bends towards. A right thoracic curve has its apex in the upper back and bends to the right.

Thoracic curves

These sit in the upper and middle back, where the ribs attach. Because rotation carries the ribs with it, thoracic curves tend to be the most visible ones: a prominence on one side of the back when bending forward, one shoulder blade sitting higher or further out, an uneven shoulder line.

Thoracic curves in a growing spine have historically been regarded as carrying a higher risk of progression than lumbar curves of the same size, which is one reason they are followed closely.

Lumbar and thoracolumbar curves

Lower curves often show differently. There are no ribs to carry, so instead of a rib hump you see an uneven waist, one crease deeper than the other, or a hip that appears higher. People often notice it in how clothes hang rather than in a mirror.

Lumbar curves matter a great deal in adulthood, because the lower back carries load and is the region where degenerative change tends to become symptomatic.

Double curves

Here there are two curves, typically one thoracic and one lumbar, bending in opposite directions. Each partly compensates for the other, so the head can sit reasonably central over the pelvis and the overall impression from behind can look more balanced than a single curve of similar size.

That apparent balance can be misleading. A double curve is two curves, and both need addressing.

Why the programme differs

Curve-specific exercise works by directing correction in particular directions at particular levels. A correction that lengthens and de-rotates one pattern can be unhelpful applied to another. This is the central reason a scoliosis programme cannot be taken off a shelf, and why an assessment has to establish the pattern before anything is prescribed.
