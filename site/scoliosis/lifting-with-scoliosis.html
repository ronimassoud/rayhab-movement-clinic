Pillar 03 · Rayhab's approach

Can you lift weights with scoliosis?

Short answer: for most people with a curve, yes. The longer answer is about how, not whether.

Reading time2 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Certified Schroth Practitioner
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • There is no good evidence that resistance training worsens an idiopathic curve.
  • Strength is what lets a correction hold up under real-world load.
  • Asymmetry needs attention: unilateral work is useful, and load tolerance may differ side to side.
  • Coaching quality matters more than exercise selection.

Where the worry comes from

The reasoning sounds sensible: a curve is a structural weakness, load makes structural things worse, so avoid load. The reasoning does not hold up. Bone and soft tissue adapt to load, and avoiding it produces a spine with less capacity, not a safer one.

There is no good evidence that appropriately coached resistance training causes idiopathic curves to progress.

Why it actively helps

A correction you can hold for thirty seconds lying down and lose the moment you pick up a bag is not much use. Strength through the trunk, hips and shoulders is what carries a correction into a day.

For adults especially, capacity is usually the limiting factor in what someone can tolerate, and building it is one of the more reliable things rehabilitation can offer.

What actually gets adjusted

  • Unilateral work. Single-arm and single-leg variations expose and address side-to-side differences that a barbell hides.
  • Position first, load second. Establish the corrected position, then add weight to it.
  • Breathing. Holding the corrective breathing pattern under load is a skill, and heavy bracing changes it. Both are taught.
  • Load tolerance may differ by side, and that is worth tracking rather than ignoring.

The lifts people ask about

Heavy overhead pressing, deadlifts and squats are not banned by default. They are loaded spinal tasks that need competent positioning, sensible progression and attention to how you tolerate them. Plenty of people with curves perform all three.

What warrants more caution is maximal loading under fatigue with poor positioning, which is a reasonable caution for anyone.

When to get it looked at

New or escalating pain with a specific lift, symptoms travelling into an arm or leg, or a sense that one side is losing capacity are all reasons to have it assessed rather than trained through.

What is not settled

Whether particular sports or loading patterns influence curve progression is not well established. Associations have been reported in some activities, but the evidence does not support telling people to stop training.
