Pillar 02 · The Schroth Method

Schroth, Pilates, yoga and the gym: what does what

Almost everyone with a curve has been told to try Pilates. It is worth being precise about what each of these actually offers.

Reading time2 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Certified Schroth Practitioner
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Schroth provides curve-specific correction. Nothing else on this list does.
  • Pilates and yoga build control, mobility and body awareness, but are not curve-specific by design.
  • Strength training builds the capacity that makes a correction holdable under load.
  • The sensible arrangement is the specific work plus general training, not one instead of the other.

What Schroth does that the others do not

It prescribes directional corrections matched to your specific curve, and teaches you to produce and hold them. No general class can do this, because the instructor is teaching a room rather than a pattern.

Pilates and yoga

Both can be genuinely useful. They develop trunk control, mobility, breathing awareness and the habit of paying attention to how you are positioned, all of which support corrective work.

The limitation is that they are not built around scoliosis. Some positions are symmetrical in a way that does not address an asymmetrical spine, and deep end-range rotation is worth discussing with whoever is managing your curve. A teacher who knows you have scoliosis and is willing to adapt is worth a great deal.

Strength training

Often the most underrated piece. A correction that cannot be held under load is a correction that exists in a clinic and nowhere else. Building strength through the trunk, hips and shoulders is what lets the correction survive a school bag, a shift at work, or a season of sport.

There is no good evidence that resistance training is inherently bad for a curved spine. Loading well matters more than avoiding load.

How to stack them

  1. The corrective programme is the non-negotiable core, done most days, kept short enough to survive real life.
  2. Strength work two or three times a week, coached, progressive, built so it does not fight the correction.
  3. Pilates, yoga or a class you enjoy, if you want it, with the instructor informed.

The order reflects priority, not importance to your week. The thing most likely to be dropped when life gets busy is the corrective work, which is exactly the thing that should not be.
