Pillar 01 · What scoliosis is

Scoliosis and pain: what the link really is

People assume a curved spine must hurt. For adolescents that assumption is usually wrong, which surprises families in both directions.

Reading time2 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Schroth Level 2
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Adolescents with idiopathic curves are frequently pain-free, and curve size does not predict pain well.
  • Back pain in adolescents is common in general, whether or not there is a curve.
  • In adults, particularly with degenerative curves, pain is a much more prominent feature.
  • Severe, night-time or neurological symptoms are not typical and should be assessed.

In adolescents

Most adolescents with idiopathic scoliosis do not have significant pain, and where pain exists it correlates poorly with how large the curve is. A 45-degree curve can be comfortable; a 20-degree curve can ache.

It is also worth knowing that back pain is common in adolescents generally, curve or no curve. Finding a curve in a teenager with back pain does not establish that the curve is the cause.

In adults

The picture changes. Adults present with scoliosis far more often because of pain and difficulty with daily activity than because of appearance or progression. In degenerative curves the pain often comes from the discs, joints and nerve spaces that are changing with age, in a spine whose alignment makes those changes less well tolerated.

For adults, this reframes the goal. The question is rarely how to change the angle and usually how to load the spine well, build capacity, and manage symptoms over years.

What tends to help

  • Building strength and endurance in the trunk and hips, so the spine is supported through a day rather than a session.
  • Learning positions and movement strategies that reduce provocation during the activities that actually cause trouble.
  • Keeping generally active. Deconditioning reliably makes spinal pain harder to manage.

When pain needs a closer look

Some patterns are not typical of a curve alone and deserve proper assessment rather than reassurance.

  • Pain that consistently wakes you at night.
  • Pain with fever, unexplained weight loss, or feeling generally unwell.
  • Numbness, weakness, or changes in bladder or bowel control.
  • A curve that appears rapidly, or pain that escalates quickly.

None of these means something serious is happening. They mean the question deserves answering properly rather than being attributed to the curve by default.
