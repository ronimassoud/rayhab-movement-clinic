Pillar 01 · What scoliosis is

Will it get worse? What actually predicts progression

This is the first question almost everyone asks, and it has a reasonably good answer, though not a certain one.

Reading time3 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Certified Schroth Practitioner
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Remaining growth is the single most important factor. A curve in a spine that has stopped growing behaves very differently.
  • Curve size matters: larger curves at a given stage of maturity are more likely to progress.
  • Curve pattern matters, with thoracic curves generally considered higher risk.
  • Risk is a probability across groups, not a prediction for one person.

Growth is the engine

Idiopathic curves overwhelmingly progress during the adolescent growth spurt, and progression usually slows markedly once growth finishes. This is why age alone is a poor guide and skeletal maturity is the useful measure: two fifteen-year-olds can be at completely different points in their growth.

Clinicians estimate remaining growth from several signs, including the Risser sign on X-ray, which grades how far a growth plate on the pelvis has matured, and from milestones such as the timing of menarche and the peak of the height spurt.

Size, at a given maturity

At the same stage of growth, a larger curve is more likely to get larger. The combination is what matters. A 30-degree curve in a child at the start of their growth spurt is watched much more closely than a 30-degree curve in someone who finished growing two years ago.

Pattern

Thoracic curves have generally been regarded as carrying a higher risk of progression than lumbar curves of equivalent size, and double curves are usually followed attentively because there are two curves that can each change.

What happens after growth stops

Most curves become substantially more stable once skeletal growth is complete. Larger curves, particularly those beyond roughly 45 to 50 degrees, may continue to change slowly over decades, which is one reason larger curves are treated more actively while there is still an opportunity.

Smaller curves in adults tend to be stable, and the reason for treating them is usually symptoms, function or appearance rather than fear of progression.

What a risk figure actually means

If you are told a curve has a high chance of progressing, that is a statement about what happens to groups of people with similar curves at a similar stage of growth. It is not a forecast for you specifically. Some high-risk curves stay put. Some low-risk curves surprise everyone.

The practical use of a risk estimate is to decide how closely to watch and how actively to intervene, not to tell you what your future is.

What is not settled

There is no test that reliably predicts an individual curve's course. Genetic tests marketed for predicting progression have not held up well under independent evaluation, and none is part of routine practice.
