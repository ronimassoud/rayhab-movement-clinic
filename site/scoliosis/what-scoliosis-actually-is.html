Pillar 01 · What scoliosis is

What scoliosis actually is

Most people picture a spine bent sideways. That picture is not wrong, but it is missing the part that matters most for treatment.

Reading time3 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Certified Schroth Practitioner
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Scoliosis is a sideways curve of 10 degrees or more, measured on a standing X-ray, combined with rotation of the vertebrae.
  • It is three-dimensional: sideways, rotated, and it changes the normal front-to-back curves too.
  • Around 2 to 3 in 100 adolescents have a curve. Most never need more than monitoring.
  • A curve is a structural finding, not a diagnosis of pain or disability.

The definition, and why it has a number in it

Scoliosis is defined as a sideways curvature of the spine measuring 10 degrees or more on a standing X-ray, together with rotation of the vertebrae. The number matters because spines are not perfectly straight. Small asymmetries are normal and extremely common. The 10-degree line exists to separate ordinary variation from something worth following.

That threshold is a convention, not a biological cliff. A curve of 9 degrees and a curve of 11 degrees are almost the same spine. What changes at that line is whether the finding gets a name and a follow-up plan.

Why it is three-dimensional

An X-ray is a flat image of a solid object. On that image a curve looks like a bend to one side. In the body, the vertebrae in that curve are also rotated, turning around the long axis of the spine, and the normal front-to-back curves of the back and lower back are usually flattened or exaggerated as well.

This is why rotation shows up on the outside of the body. When the vertebrae turn, they carry the ribs with them, which is what produces the raised area on one side of the back that becomes obvious when someone bends forward.

  • Sideways, the part the Cobb angle measures.
  • Rotational, the part that produces a rib hump or a waist that looks uneven.
  • Front-to-back, where the normal curves of the spine are altered.

Treatment that only addresses the sideways component is working on one dimension of a three-dimensional problem. That is the reasoning behind curve-specific exercise approaches, which try to act on all three.

How common it is

Roughly 2 to 3 in every 100 adolescents have a curve of 10 degrees or more. Far fewer progress to a size where active treatment is considered. Most curves found during a school or family check turn out to be small, stable, and require nothing beyond periodic review.

Curves are found more often in girls, and the difference grows with curve size: small curves are distributed fairly evenly between boys and girls, while larger curves are considerably more common in girls.

What a curve is not

A curve is a structural description. On its own it does not tell you that you will be in pain, that you will lose function, or that you will need surgery. Adolescents with curves are frequently symptom-free, and many adults live a full life with a curve they have had for decades.

It also does not tell you what caused it. In around eight out of ten cases no cause is identified at all, which is what the word idiopathic means.

What is not settled

The causes of idiopathic scoliosis are still not understood. Genetics, growth, neuromuscular control and connective tissue have all been investigated, and none of them explains the condition on its own. Anyone who tells you confidently why your curve appeared is going beyond what is currently known.
