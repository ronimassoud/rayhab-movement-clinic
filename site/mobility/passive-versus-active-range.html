Mobility & joint health

Range you can reach versus range you can control

Being able to be put into a position is not the same as being able to get there. The second one is what your nervous system will actually use.

Reading time3 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Certified Mobility Specialist (FRCms)
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Passive range is how far a joint can be moved by something else. Active range is how far you can move it yourself.
  • The gap between them is range you have access to but no control over, and it is where many strains happen.
  • Stretching generally improves passive range. Closing the gap requires strength at end range.
  • Active range is the number that transfers into sport, lifting and daily life.

Two numbers, not one

Every joint has two meaningful limits. Passive range is how far it goes when someone else, or gravity, or your other hand, moves it. Active range is how far you can move it under your own power, with nothing helping.

Most people only ever hear about the first. It is the one measured in a flexibility test and the one that improves with stretching. It is also the less useful of the two.

Why the gap matters

If your hip can be pushed to 120 degrees of flexion but you can only actively lift the leg to 95, you have 25 degrees of range you can enter but not control. That territory is reached by momentum, by a fall, by an opponent, or by a sport that takes you there, and in it you have little ability to produce force or resist it.

This is one of the more plausible explanations for strains occurring at end range, and it is why gaining passive range without the strength to use it can leave you no better protected than before.

What this looks like in an assessment

Both numbers get taken, joint by joint, side by side. The useful outputs are the difference between active and passive on the same side, and the difference between left and right, because an asymmetry you can control matters much less than one you cannot.

  • A large active-to-passive gap suggests a strength and control problem at end range.
  • A small gap with a low total suggests a genuine range limitation, which may be tissue, joint shape or bone.
  • A large gap on one side only is often the most informative finding of the session.

How the gap is closed

Not by more stretching, which tends to widen it. The work is strength in the last part of the range: holding positions you have just reached, producing force there, and gradually loading them.

  1. Reach the end of your current active range deliberately and slowly, without momentum.
  2. Hold it and contract hard, which is where most of the adaptation comes from.
  3. Add light load once the position is reliable, and keep the load light for longer than feels necessary.
  4. Re-measure every few weeks. Active range is a slow number and it does move.

What is not settled

The idea that a large passive-to-active gap increases injury risk is biologically reasonable and not strongly established by prospective evidence. Stretching alone has a poor record for injury prevention; strength training has a better one. Whether the mechanism is the control gap specifically or simply greater strength is not resolved.
