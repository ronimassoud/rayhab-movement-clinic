Mobility & joint health

Controlled articular rotations: what they are for

A CAR takes ninety seconds, involves no equipment, and is one of the few mobility drills that tells you something about the joint while you do it.

Reading time3 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Certified Mobility Specialist (FRCms)
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • A controlled articular rotation is the largest circle a joint can make actively, performed slowly with the rest of the body still.
  • They are used as both a training input and an assessment: how the circle feels maps where control is missing.
  • They are a core element of Functional Range Conditioning, the system behind the FRCms credential.
  • The evidence base is largely mechanistic and clinical rather than trial-based, and the honest framing says so.

What a CAR actually is

You take one joint through the largest circle it can make, actively, as slowly as you can manage, with high tension in the surrounding muscles and with the rest of the body deliberately prevented from helping. A shoulder CAR means the shoulder moves and the ribcage and spine do not.

That last constraint is what makes it difficult. Most people's large shoulder circles are mostly spine. Removing the compensation reveals the real range, which is usually smaller and less even than expected.

What it is used for

  • Maintaining range. A daily input that asks each joint to visit its available range under its own power, which ordinary life and most training do not.
  • Assessment. The circle is not smooth in the same places for everyone. Hesitation, skipping, pain or loss of control in one arc localises where the work is needed.
  • Preparation. A sensible way into a session, because it warms the joint without fatiguing anything.
  • Monitoring. Doing the same circle daily makes change, in either direction, noticeable early.

Doing them properly

  1. One joint at a time, and keep everything else still. Use a wall or a mirror if you cannot tell.
  2. Go slowly. Thirty seconds for a full circle is reasonable; faster is almost always compensating.
  3. Generate tension through the whole range, roughly half of your maximum effort, rather than drifting round loosely.
  4. Stay out of pain. These should be effortful, not provocative.
  5. Two to three circles per direction, daily. Volume is not the variable here; consistency is.

Where this sits in the wider system

Controlled articular rotations come from Functional Range Conditioning, a system built around active range, end-range strength and joint capacity. The other components include progressive and regressive angular isometric loading, which is the end-range strength work described elsewhere in this hub, and loaded stretching protocols.

Used sensibly it is a coherent framework for training joints rather than only muscles. Used as marketing it makes larger claims about joint health and longevity than anyone can currently support.

What is not settled

Functional Range Conditioning has little direct trial evidence. Its components rest on reasonable principles, including active range mattering more than passive and tissue adapting to load, and the specific protocols have not been tested against alternatives in controlled studies. Claims about preventing arthritis or maintaining joint health over decades are not established. We use it because it is a useful, low-risk structure for joint work, and we say plainly that the proof is principle and practice rather than trials.
