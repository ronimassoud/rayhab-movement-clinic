Mobility & joint health

Hypermobility: more range is not better

The most flexible people in the room are frequently the ones in the most discomfort, which makes very little sense until you separate range from control.

Reading time3 min read
Written byRayan Halimeh, Certified Mobility Specialist (FRCms)
StatusAwaiting clinical review

In short

  • Hypermobility means joints move beyond the usual range, often from inherited differences in connective tissue.
  • It is common, frequently harmless, and sometimes part of a broader condition that needs proper diagnosis.
  • Hypermobile people often have poor control at end range despite having plenty of it, and a worse sense of joint position.
  • The training priority is strength and stability, not stretching, which is the opposite of what feels natural.

What hypermobility is

Joints that move further than typical, usually because of inherited differences in collagen and connective tissue. It is often screened with a simple nine-point scale covering elbows, knees, thumbs, little fingers and the trunk. It is common, more so in women and in younger people, and it frequently causes no trouble at all.

Where it does cause trouble, the presentation can include joint pain, a history of sprains or dislocations, fatigue, and a sense that joints feel unstable or slip. Some people have a broader hypermobility spectrum disorder or a hereditary connective tissue condition, and those warrant a proper medical diagnosis rather than an assumption.

Why more range can mean more pain

Range without control is the recurring theme of this hub, and hypermobility is the extreme case of it. A joint that travels a long way needs a great deal of active control to manage that territory, and the usual pattern is a large passive range with relatively weak end-range strength.

Proprioception, the sense of where a joint is in space, also tends to be less accurate in hypermobile people. The combination of a large range, reduced control and a less reliable internal sense of position explains a lot of the symptoms without needing any tissue damage at all.

What training should look like

Almost the reverse of the instinct. Flexible people enjoy stretching, are good at it, and generally need very little of it.

  1. Strength, through range, loaded. This is the priority and it is not optional.
  2. Work within a controlled range rather than at the far end. Resist the urge to demonstrate the full range.
  3. Isometric holds in mid and end positions, to build control where control is missing.
  4. Slow, deliberate tempo, because speed and momentum are how hypermobile joints get to places they cannot manage.
  5. Reduce stretching, which usually feels good briefly and adds nothing you need.
  6. Progress slowly. Hypermobile people often tolerate less load initially than their flexibility suggests, and get flare-ups from large jumps.

When to get it properly diagnosed

If hypermobility comes with recurrent dislocations, significant fatigue, skin that bruises or scars unusually, digestive symptoms, or cardiovascular symptoms, that combination deserves medical assessment rather than a training plan alone. Hereditary connective tissue disorders are managed by a team, and physiotherapy is one part of it rather than the whole.

What is not settled

The relationship between hypermobility and pain is not fully explained, and many hypermobile people have no symptoms at all. Classification of hypermobility spectrum disorders has changed in recent years and continues to be refined. Strength-based management is the consensus approach and the trial evidence supporting specific protocols is limited.
