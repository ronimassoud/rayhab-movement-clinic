The approach

A curve is not a line on an X-ray

Scoliosis involves rotation, alignment, posture, breathing mechanics and individual movement patterns at the same time. Rehabilitation here works on all of them, in the order that makes sense for your spine.

Three-dimensional correction

Elongation, de-rotation and stabilisation worked together rather than in isolation.

Rotational breathing

Directed breathing strategies that reach the concave side of the curve.

Carried into daily life

Corrections rehearsed in sitting, walking, lifting and sport, not only on a mat.

Flow decisions

Request, not instant booking

A single-clinician practice can't safely auto-confirm. Saying so up front removes the biggest source of post-booking anxiety.

"For my child" changes nothing structural

It only relabels the patient fields and adds a date-of-birth field at build time. Parents are a primary audience, so the flow acknowledges them in step 1.

No login, no payment

Both are friction the clinic doesn't need yet. The data model still assumes a future patient portal.

Validation is on continue, not on keystroke

Errors name the fix, never just "invalid". The rail summary stays visible so nothing feels lost.

Mobile keeps the same four steps

The rail becomes a progress bar and the summary moves into a fixed bottom bar carrying the running choice.

Why the approach differs

Scoliosis is not a line on an X-ray

It is rotation, alignment, posture, breathing mechanics and an individual movement pattern, all at once. Treating only the curve misses most of the problem.

Individually assessed

Your curve pattern decides the exercises. There is no standard sheet handed to every patient.

Actively rehabilitated

You do the work, coached. Passive treatment alone does not change how a spine loads.

Measured, not guessed

Posture, rotation and function are re-measured at review points so progress is visible.

Built to hand over

The aim is a programme you can run yourself, with periodic reviews — not indefinite appointments.

Misconceptions

Things patients are told that aren't true

Correcting these is the fastest way to earn trust, and it is what people actually search for. Each one links to the full explanation.

Myth

"Nothing can be done until it gets bad enough for surgery."

Specific corrective exercise, postural training and monitoring are active options long before that point. Waiting is a choice, not the only path.

Myth

"Exercise is dangerous with a curve."

Unstructured exercise may not help, but strength, mobility and loading are usually part of good management — chosen for your pattern.

Myth

"Bad posture and heavy schoolbags caused it."

Most adolescent scoliosis is idiopathic. Blaming posture or bags shifts guilt onto families and changes nothing clinically.

Myth

"Once you stop growing, it's too late."

Adult rehabilitation goals differ from adolescent ones, but strength, function, breathing mechanics and pain are all still addressable.

Every claim on these pages stays inside what the evidence supports. Where the research is genuinely uncertain, the article says so rather than filling the gap with confidence.

Reading your Cobb angle

Reviewed by Rayan Halimeh

Schroth L2 · updated Aug 2026

The Cobb angle is the measurement your report leads with, and it is the number most conversations get stuck on. It describes the size of the curve on a single two-dimensional image.

In short

A Cobb angle tells you how large the curve is. It does not tell you how rotated the spine is, how it moves, or how it will behave next year.

What it leaves out matters just as much. Rotation, flexibility, muscular control and breathing mechanics are not visible in that single figure, and they are a large part of what rehabilitation works on.

The angle is measured between the most tilted vertebra above and below the curve — a flat description of a three-dimensional shape.
